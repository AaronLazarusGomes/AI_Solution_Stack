From Exposure to Productivity Impact

How to interpret AI sensitivity scores for workforce planning and pricing

Building on the task-level exposure data

Across all 600 tasks analyzed, 84% have at least one AI capability flag. But exposure does not mean replacement. We can categorize each task by the depth of AI impact to understand what changes and how:

THREE IMPACT CATEGORIES

16%96 tasks

Human-Led

Tasks with no AI capability flags. Require judgment, advocacy, client relationships, or accountability that cannot be delegated.

Invest in developing these skills. They become the firm’s differentiator as routine work is automated.

13%78 tasks

AI-Augmented

Tasks touched by a single AI capability. The lawyer remains fully in the loop while AI improves speed or accuracy.

Target for near-term productivity gains and the first candidates for fixed-fee pricing.

71%426 tasks

AI-Transformed

Tasks intersecting 2+ AI capabilities. These face the deepest change as multiple AI tools converge, though the human remains in the loop and accountable.

Requires redesigning how the work is staffed, supervised and priced. Prioritize in leverage model reviews.

FIVE PRIORITY SKILL THEMES

Analysis of human-led tasks reveals five recurring skill themes that grow in importance as AI absorbs routine legal work:

1

Judgment in Novel and Ambiguous Matters

Forming and defending a view where precedent is unsettled, facts are contested and the commercial stakes of being wrong are material.

2

Client Trust and Commercial Counsel

Understanding the client’s business, framing legal risk in commercial terms and holding difficult conversations well.

3

Advocacy, Negotiation and Persuasion

Arguing a position before courts, regulators and counterparties, where outcomes turn on credibility, timing and read of the room.

4

Professional Ethics and Accountability

Owning conflicts, privilege and duty-to-court decisions, and verifying AI output before it carries the firm’s name.

5

Supervision and Talent Development

Reviewing and signing off work, and deliberately building judgment in junior lawyers as routine training work is automated.

Implications for the Leverage Model

The work that traditionally trained junior lawyers is the work most exposed to AI. Firms must rebuild how judgment is acquired — or face a capability gap at the senior end within a decade.
